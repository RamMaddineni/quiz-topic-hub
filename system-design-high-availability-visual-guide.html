High availability means failures do not become long outages.

Machines, networks, and dependencies will fail. An available design limits how much of the service is lost and how long recovery takes. The complete mechanism is redundancy + failure detection + traffic or data failover—not simply “run two copies.”

What the percentage does not say

A yearly 99.9% target does not tell you whether one 8-hour outage is acceptable or whether every incident must recover within 30 seconds. State both the aggregate target and the acceptable duration of one incident.

Availability is not durability

Availability asks, “Can users use the service now?” Durability asks, “Will stored data survive over time?” A read-only copy can keep a page available while the write path is unavailable; a reachable system can still corrupt data.

What must happen after a component fails

This example spans two Availability Zones. An Availability Zone (AZ) is an isolated cloud location inside one geographic region, designed so a failure in one zone does not automatically fail the other.

Why the load balancer also needs redundancy

If one ordinary load-balancer machine is the only public entry point, its failure hides every healthy application behind it. A managed load balancer normally presents one logical endpoint while the provider runs redundant nodes. A self-managed design needs multiple balancers plus a way for the public address to reach a healthy one.

What this still does not guarantee

Failover is not necessarily instant or lossless. Health checks can be too slow or too sensitive. An asynchronously replicated standby may be missing the latest writes. The surviving zone can also overload if it was not provisioned to absorb the failed zone’s traffic.

Decision rule: for every box whose failure would violate the availability target, identify a redundant path, the detector, the failover action, the expected recovery time, and the capacity remaining after failover.

Place copies outside the failure you want to survive

A failure domain is a group of resources that can fail together. Two copies in the same domain are redundant for a process crash, but not for the shared failure.
